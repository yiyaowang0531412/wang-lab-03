3. Specify and estimate competing models

We expect that better handling of employee complaints will be positively associated with overall workplace ratings, as effective complaint resolution may improve employees’ perceptions of management. Additionally, opportunities for learning may contribute to higher workplace ratings by supporting professional development. Finally, pay increases may explain additional variation in workplace ratings, as financial rewards could contribute to employee satisfaction.

Code 
model_performance <- bind_rows(
  glance(m0) %>%
    mutate(Model = "M0",
           RMSE = sqrt(mean(residuals(m0)^2))),

  glance(m1) %>%
    mutate(Model = "M1",
           RMSE = sqrt(mean(residuals(m1)^2))),

  glance(m2) %>%
    mutate(Model = "M2",
           RMSE = sqrt(mean(residuals(m2)^2))),

  glance(m3) %>%
    mutate(Model = "M3",
           RMSE = sqrt(mean(residuals(m3)^2)))
) %>%
  select(
    Model,
    R2 = r.squared,
    Adjusted_R2 = adj.r.squared,
    RMSE
  )

Model comparisons revealed that M3 explained the largest proportion of variance in workplace ratings (R² = .708), although M2 had the highest adjusted R² (.686). Adding complaint handling significantly improved model fit compared with the null model (ΔR² = .681, F(1, 28) = 60.73, p < .001). 6. Reporting preferred model

M2 was selected as the preferred model because it provided the highest adjusted R² (.686) and a relatively low RMSE (6.467) while maintaining a parsimonious specification. Compared with M1, the addition of learning opportunities increased explained variance by 2.67 percentage points and modestly improved prediction accuracy, although the improvement was not statistically significant (p = .135). M3 provided virtually no additional explanatory benefit and introduced unnecessary complexity. Despite minor concerns identified during diagnostic evaluation, M2 offers a reasonable balance between explanatory power, simplicity, and substantive interpretability.

Holding learning opportunities constant, a one-unit increase in complaint handling was associated with a 0.644-unit increase in overall workplace ratings (95% CI [0.400, 0.887], p < .001). This positive association was statistically significant, and the confidence interval excluded zero.

Holding complaint handling constant, a one-unit increase in learning opportunities was associated with a 0.211-unit increase in workplace ratings (95% CI [-0.065, 0.487], p = 0.128). However, this association was not statistically significant, and the confidence interval included zero, indicating uncertainty about the unique contribution of learning opportunities.

The preferred model (M2) explained 70.8% of the variance in overall workplace ratings (R² = 0.708). The adjusted R² was 0.686, indicating that the model maintained substantial explanatory power after accounting for the number of predictors. The RMSE was 6.467, indicating a typical prediction error magnitude of approximately 6.467 rating points, measured using root mean squared error. Overall, the model demonstrated reasonably strong explanatory performance.

The results suggest that complaint handling is the strongest predictor of overall workplace ratings among the three variables examined. Complaint handling alone explained approximately 68.1% of the variance in workplace ratings. Learning opportunities explained an additional 2.7% of variance, although this improvement was not statistically significant. Pay increases contributed virtually no additional explanatory power after accounting for complaint handling and learning opportunities. These findings suggest that employees’ perceptions of how complaints are handled may be more strongly associated with overall workplace evaluations than opportunities for learning or pay increases.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the dataset contains only 30 departments, limiting statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Second, because the data are observational and aggregated at the department level, causal conclusions cannot be drawn, and the findings may not reflect relationships at the individual employee level. Third, the diagnostic analysis identified potential heteroscedasticity and an influential observation, which may affect the reliability of the regression estimates. Future research using larger samples and additional workplace variables could provide a more comprehensive understanding of factors associated with workplace ratings.
